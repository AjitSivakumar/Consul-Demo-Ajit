Why Bridgeport data is structurally thinner -- grounded in CT data
01
Bridgeport has fewer census tracts and smaller per-tract birth populations than New Haven. Bridgeport (pop. ~148K) and New Haven (pop. ~135K) are comparable in size, but New Haven's population is concentrated across a smaller geographic footprint with denser tract-level birth counts -- giving the Chen Lab data more statistical power per tract. At census tract level (typically 1,200--8,000 residents), this means New Haven tracts generate more births per observation window, reducing uncertainty in effect estimates. Source: DataHaven Connecticut City Neighborhood Profiles; US Census Bureau QuickFacts CT. ctdatahaven.org -- Bridgeport profiles
02
Bridgeport's Q4 income quartile (highest income tracts) has very few births relative to its lower-income tracts, making income-stratified estimates especially unstable in the upper quartile. The DataHaven 2021 New Haven Equity Profile and Bridgeport Equity data show that Bridgeport's income distribution is heavily skewed toward lower-income tracts -- meaning Q4 estimates are based on a very small number of births. This is precisely the stratum Sarah flagged as most at risk in the preprint data. DataHaven New Haven Equity Profile (2021)
03
CT DPH's own surveillance data acknowledges sample suppression in smaller geographic units. The CT-LLoVE longitudinal dataset (maternal-birth linkage 2010--2020) notes that small cell sizes in specific census tracts require suppression to prevent re-identification, particularly for less common outcomes like preterm birth in specific income-heat exposure combinations. This structural limitation applies more acutely to Bridgeport, where tract-level birth counts in specific exposure quintiles fall near or below standard suppression thresholds. Source: CT DPH MCH Data Capacity reporting to HRSA (2023). CT DPH MCH Data Capacity -- HRSA 2023
